Under the Block

One delivery dock, one lane under the plaza, four buildings to serve.

A fictional micro-freight network in one live model. Deliveries queue at a dock, drop one storey into a single-lane service trench under the plaza, and ride up into four tenant buildings, while outgoing mail flows the other way through the same lane. The page is one HTML file with its renderer, physics and camera written in, so it loads nothing from a CDN and opens the same way offline or online. The first part is the model in section; the second hands the routing to a proposer, which the model accepts or refuses.

Part 1

Movement Through the Whole System

Every cart is one continuous object for its whole trip: curb, dock, shaft, trench, passing bay, building lift, lobby. The front of the block is cut away like a section model, so the trench reads from the street side.

1 · The trench in section — dock to lobby

built-in WebGL 2 · no CDN ✓

A cart waits under the dock canopy, rolls into the dock headhouse, rides the platform down and is set beside the shaft. From there it runs west along the one-lane trench. Carts heading the opposite way can only pass each other where a passing bay widens the lane to two. At its building the cart rides the glass lift up to street level and rolls through the covered link into the lobby. Nothing is scripted as a path. Each cart follows the cell the queueing simulation says it occupies, carried by gravity and solid floors.

How to play

How to play. Every robot in the trench carries a small button: ❚❚ stops it where it stands, ▶ sends it on. Two robots meeting head-on in the one-lane trench jam it, so stop one in a passing lane until the other is through, or press + Add passing lane to widen a cell (during a jam it is built where they are stuck, and they pass each other in it). Start challenge empties the trench, removes every lane and asks for 12 deliveries: three stars for two lanes or fewer with no jam and nothing left at the curb.

Parameters
Dock
Five painted pads under a glass canopy. Peak arrivals follow a one-hour morning wave. A sixth waiting cart has nowhere to stand: it goes onto red pads at the curb, and that is the failure the whole model is built to avoid.
Dock lift

A cell widened to 3.4 m that holds two carts side by side. The slider adds bays at choke points first, then where traffic is heaviest. Now and then a cart pauses in a bay (grey) before going on.

Prompt to build it
Build one self-contained index.html with no libraries and nothing loaded from the web. Write a small WebGL 2 renderer (boxes, cylinders and spheres with real thickness, sun and sky light, soft shadow maps, glass, fog), a small rigid-body physics step (gravity, damping, rotation-locked boxes resolved against static floors) and an orbit camera. Model a 17-cell, one-lane service trench 2.3 m below a plaza: a dock with five waiting pads, a dock lift, four tenant buildings each with its own glass lift headhouse, and passing bays that widen a cell to hold two carts. Drive it with a deterministic queueing simulation in cells and minutes: wave-shaped arrivals, a single-server dock lift, one cart per cell, a wait-for-graph deadlock check. The physics only decides where each cart visibly sits. Cut away the front of the block to show the trench in section. Put every number in a settings block at the top with its source in a comment.

Part 2

What the Model Learns, and How It Refuses

The toy watches itself. When something goes wrong in the live run (a surge fills the dock, carts spill onto the curb, the trench jams) an alert appears in the corner of the model with a Suggest next move button. Pressing it sends a proposal to the model: a routing (the repeating order in which new carts are sent to tenants A–D) and a number of passing bays. The model plays the proposal through two full morning waves before accepting it. If a cart would end up at the curb or the trench would jam, the proposal is refused and the failure plays out on the board, not just as a score.

2 · Refusal you can see

shown, not just scored ✓

A refused move is not a red line of text. The board above switches to a replay of that move, sped up and starting from an empty morning, using the same steps the validator used, so it fails at exactly the minute reported. Then it stops on the failure:

Jam
The trench cells involved glow red under the stuck carts. The carts in the wait-for loop turn red, and a label over the trench says how many are waiting on each other.
Curb
The red curb pads flash and the overflowing carts sit on them in red: the dock lift could not keep up with the morning wave.
Banner
States the refusal in words: what failed, at which minute, and why. Back to live run returns to the live run, which was paused underneath. The same red marks appear when the live run itself jams or spills onto the curb, and that is when the alert and its Suggest button show up.

Two ways to see a refusal. Press Try a risky move under the model (no passing bays, most carts to the far tenant). Or push the arrivals slider to its top, wait for the alert, and press Suggest next move: when no move can survive that load, the model puts forward the one that lasts longest anyway, and refuses it.

Lessons from this session's proposals

Found behavior
Under the Block (Service Stratum · flow)
5 dock pads, one lift, one one-lane trench, up to 13 passing bays; you stop and release robots and add passing lanes while it runs; the model proposes routing and bay edits between rounds; jams flash red. Found behavior: sending more carts to the near tenants beats adding bays. At the default 16 arrivals/hr, the even round-robin needs 7 passing bays to survive, while a routing weighted toward the near tenants ([0,0,1,2,3]) survives with none, at the same 10 deliveries/hr. Short trips keep fewer carts in the lane at once, so fewer carts meet head-on.
Sibling toy · Corridor Commons (Service Stratum · flow)
12 bots, one lobby, three door widths; the model proposes plan edits between rounds; jams flash. Found behavior: two narrow one-way loops beat one generous corridor.

The two found behaviors rhyme: in both, the winning plan is the one that stops traffic from meeting head-on, not the one that gives it the most room.

How it works 
after any slider or routing change (debounced)
    for each of 12 routings × every passing-bay count 0..13
        dry-run it 120 simulated minutes; keep: survived?, minute of failure, deliveries/hr,
        and for a jam, which carts (inbound / outgoing) and which cells were stuck
    efficiency   ← minutes survived per bay count, current routing
    jam anatomy  ← the kinds of cart and the cells, counted over every failed run

    routing      ← per routing, the fewest bays that survive; ranked, with average trip length

Part 3 · Known result

Known Result: Little's Law

Before this toy is trusted with real dock counts, it has to reproduce a result that is already known. Queueing theory gives one that fits a loading dock exactly, and it holds whatever the routing, the trench or the arrival pattern, as long as carts can get out.

L = λ × W
The average number of carts inside the system equals the rate they arrive times the average time each one spends inside. A corollary: when carts arrive faster than the dock lift can take them (ρ = λ / μ ≥ 1) the queue never clears, and grows by about λ − μ carts every hour.

running the check…
L
Carts inside the system, averaged over time: on the dock pads, on the dock lift, waiting below it, or travelling the trench.
λ (lambda)
Arrival rate: carts that joined the dock queue, divided by minutes run.
W
Average minutes from joining the dock queue to reaching the tenant's cell, over every delivered cart.
μ (mu)
What the dock lift can take: 60 ÷ lift cycle carts per hour (30/hr at the 2-minute default).
ρ (rho)
λ ÷ μ. Below 1 the queue clears between arrivals; at or above 1 it cannot.
The small gap
W only counts carts that have finished, so carts still inside when you look make λ × W run a little behind L. The gap shrinks as the run gets longer and disappears when the block empties.
A large gap
Means carts are stuck and cannot leave: the trench has jammed. That is the one-lane trench refusing, not the law failing, and it is why the sweep runs the dock lift alone as well as the full trench.
How it works 
every simulation step dt
    area      += carts inside × dt                 the integral of L(t)
    a cart joins the dock queue   → note its arrival minute, arrivals += 1
    a cart reaches its tenant     → W_sum += now − arrival minute, delivered += 1
on screen
    L = area / T      λ = arrivals / T      W = W_sum / delivered
    toy value = L     textbook value = λ × W     gap = (λW − L) / L
    μ = 60 / lift cycle      ρ = 60 λ / μ
